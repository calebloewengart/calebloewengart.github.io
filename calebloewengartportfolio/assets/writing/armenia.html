Examining the Nagorno-Karabakh Conflict in the Post-Cold War Era: An Intercultural Communication Lens

Academic Analysis | International Relations & Conflict Studies

Summary

The conflict in Nagorno-Karabakh begins with the establishment of the region as an autonomous oblast within Azerbaijan's Soviet Socialist Republic. This oblast was majority Armenian, thus when the USSR began to show signs of collapse, the regional legislature passed a resolution declaring its intention to join the Republic of Armenia. Prior to this, the borders of Nagorno-Karabakh were of less concern, as the USSR was one country, and its citizens could freely travel across regions. However, with the collapse of the Soviet Union, the ethnic tensions between Armenians and Azerbaijanis were reignited and it became more difficult for ethnic Armenians to travel from the Karabakh to Armenia, as they share no borders. In 1988, three years before the USSR fell, the first Nagorno-Karabakh war began. The conflict started as a series of small skirmishes, developing into a full-scale war in 1991. The war resulted in 30,000 casualties and hundreds of thousands of refugees. By 1993, Armenia had gained full control of Nagorno-Karabakh and 20% of the surrounding Azerbaijani territory. The war ended in 1994 with a ceasefire that was formally held until 2020, leaving Nagorno-Karabakh as a de-facto independent state, but it was heavily reliant on Armenia for resources and military support.

Between 1994 and 2020 there were intermittent clashes between Armenia and Azerbaijan, with drones and special operations providing an ever-present reminder that war could break out at any moment. The ceasefire formally ended in 2020 with a six-week war. Azerbaijan regained most of the territory it had lost in 1993, leaving Armenia with only a small portion of Karabakh. In the ceasefire that ended this war, a corridor was established between Nagorno-Karabakh and Armenia to transport materials and aid to the ethnic Armenians in the region. This highway, the Lachin corridor, was to be monitored by Russian peacekeepers. However, violence continued to insinuate. In 2022, government-backed Azerbaijani activists blocked the corridor to everyone except for the Red Cross and Russian envoys in protest of environmental degradation due to mining in Nagorno-Karabakh. These protests led to severe shortages and rationing for the people living in the region. Azerbaijan tightened Armenian access to the region with the opening of a checkpoint in April 2023. The protests stopped soon after the checkpoint was put up, leading to speculation that the true intention of the protesters was solely to limit the flow of goods through the corridor. After the checkpoint was put up, Nagorno-Karabakh was effectively cut off from Armenia. The Red Cross wasn't allowed in for fear of smuggling weapons and ethnic Armenians seeking medical care weren't able to pass over to Armenia.

Days after agreeing to re-open the Lachin Corridor in September 2023, Azerbaijan launched an offensive into Nagorno-Karabakh killing around 200 people. Their aim was to neutralize the Armenian military hold in the area. Following this attack, Azerbaijan reassumed full control of the region and about 100,000 Armenians are documented as having fled. It's estimated that there are somewhere between 50 and 1000 ethnic Armenians remaining in Nagorno-Karabakh. The regional leaders of Nagorno-Karabakh were arrested and are awaiting trial in Baku.

Origins

The protracted conflict in Nagorno-Karabakh is representative of a larger global trend towards longer conflicts that come about due to ethnic, religious, or post-colonial tensions. Nagorno-Karabakh is an example of a division made by an external power that led to an increase in tensions in the wake of the power's collapse. The Karabakh was divided by the USSR along ethnic lines, but there was no connection between the region and Armenia. Following the collapse of the Soviet Union, Armenians' and Azeris' freedom of mobility was limited, producing tensions between the ethnic Armenians and Azeris who lived in Nagorno-Karabakh, a majority Armenian enclave in Azerbaijan.

Upon initial examination of Azeri and Armenian values, it appears as if there would be little justification for conflict between the two nations. Both are traditional, hospitable nations and Azerbaijan's propensity towards religious tolerance should allow the two to coexist well enough. However, the strong ties to the land in Nagorno-Karabakh, which has been under control of both states throughout history, makes the issue of land indivisibility one that supersedes any values that may have limited causes for war in another case.

Types of Conflict

One of the types of conflict at play between Azerbaijan and Armenia is a goal conflict. Goal conflicts occur when there is disagreement about a preferred outcome or end state. Because Azerbaijan and Armenia are both trying to exercise sovereignty over the same region, and that is not possible, they are in conflict over incompatible outcomes. The land divisions made by the Soviet Union limited short-term potential for a conflict but didn't anticipate the collapse of the state. The lack of land borders with Armenia exacerbated the tensions between ethnic Armenians and Azeris because travel between Nagorno-Karabakh and Armenia was made more difficult. There is also an issue of land indivisibility, because Nagorno-Karabakh is internationally recognized as part of Azerbaijan, hence under Azeri sovereignty, but there is a large portion of the population who rejects that rule and wants to be independent or under Armenian control.

There is also an affective conflict in Nagorno-Karabakh, where feelings or emotions expressed by the two groups are incompatible. Because the Armenian majority in the region didn't want to be ruled by Azerbaijan, their feelings were incompatible with the Azeri national identity which they were subject to. This is expressed through the regional council's interest in becoming part of Armenia following the collapse of the USSR, which wasn't brought to fruition. Now that most ethnic Armenians have left the region, this conflict may have subsided, but it's still an important factor to explain why the conflict has been fought for so long. Furthermore, because both states feel a strong historical connection to Nagorno-Karabakh, they're unwilling to relinquish their control and sever that connection. Since retaking control of the region, Azerbaijan has been removing Armenian monuments, churches, and cemeteries, indicating that Armenian feelings towards Nagorno-Karabakh are incompatible with Azerbaijanis' and may even be considered a threat.

Forces Influencing the Conflict

The main forces in this conflict are social and historical. The ethnic divisions between Armenians and Azeris have created strong senses of national identity that have led to clashes over mutual claim to the same region. Armenians are a majority in Nagorno-Karabakh but a persecuted minority in the rest of Azerbaijan. The Azeris have limited the ability of Armenians in the Karabakh to access the resources they need to survive, indicating strong social tensions between the two groups. The separation between Armenians and Azeris in the Karabakh, regardless of whether it's voluntary creates an environment wherein tensions can fester. These divisions allow each group to form an opinion about the other that can turn negative. Both groups have strong ties to the region, making it difficult to relinquish, hence, they are going to form negative opinions about the other group who is trying to control the same land. Therefore, the conflict is exacerbated by the strained relationship of Armenian and Azeri neighbors in Nagorno-Karabakh.

Another force that significantly affects this conflict is the historical one. The basis for this conflict is over 100 years old, thus, there's been a long time for these emotions to fester and for the historical grievances to accumulate.
